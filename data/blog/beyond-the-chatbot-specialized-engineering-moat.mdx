In my free time, I closely monitor stock market trends and value investing. Recently, following analysis by financial YouTuber Joseph Carlson, one of the most volatile stocks on his portfolio caught my eye: Duolingo.

As you can see in the chart below, the stock has plummeted over 60 percent over the past year, dropping from a peak of nearly 370 USD down to around 135 USD. This massive sell off is driven by a growing debate over whether it is an AI victim stock. Investors fear general purpose AI will commoditize language learning. After all, why would anyone pay for a structured app when a baseline LLM can translate and converse for free?

![Duolingo (NASDAQ: DUOL) stock chart showing a 60% drop over the past year, from a peak near 370 USD down to about 135 USD](/static/images/duolingo-stock.png)

We saw exactly how this plays out when a tech company stays stagnant. Look at the timeline of monthly questions asked on Stack Overflow. Traffic was completely stable for years, but the moment ChatGPT launched, engagement fell off a cliff, dropping rapidly from over 100,000 monthly questions down toward 25,000. Users realized they could get instant code debugging directly from an LLM instead of waiting for forum replies. They failed to adapt to a fundamental shift in user behavior.

![Chart of monthly questions asked on Stack Overflow, peaking around 2014-2017 and declining sharply after ChatGPT launches](/static/images/stackoverflow-questions.jpg)

But looking at Duolingo, I believe they are actively fighting not to be the next Stack Overflow. By launching Duolingo Max powered by OpenAI GPT4 for roleplaying and answer explanations, they are leaning directly into disruption.

A huge piece of this puzzle is the target audience. The core demographic of these apps consists of everyday people, not software engineers. They might not be proficient in prompt engineering or know how to instruct a generic LLM to act as a proper tutor. There is massive value in designing a highly specified, customized learning robot that does all the heavy lifting for them.

From an engineering view, building a true moat requires going beyond basic text wrappers. Here is how a specialized platform can out engineer a generic chat interface:

- **Frustration Detection:** Architectures running real time sentiment analysis to detect when a user is losing motivation, instantly adjusting the lesson difficulty before they close the app.
- **Regional Accent Mastery and Waveform Feedback:** A generic text bot cannot teach you how to sound like a local. Apps need multimodal audio LLMs with near zero latency to deliver dynamic phonetic corrections. Going beyond audio generation, the app could analyze the waveform of the user speech and visually show them where their tongue placement is incorrect compared to a native speaker.
- **Visual Immersion:** A feature where users point their camera at their surroundings, and the AI uses computer vision to instantly generate a custom vocabulary lesson based entirely on the objects in their immediate environment.
- **Dynamic Spaced Repetition Storytelling:** Instead of just reminding users to review old words, a specialized AI could dynamically generate a short audio story every morning that perfectly weaves in the exact vocabulary words the user struggled with the day before.
- **Situational Stress Testing:** Roleplaying highly specific cultural scenarios. For example, programming a situation like buying a coffee from an impatient barista in a busy Madrid cafe. There are endless different situations that could be programmed to force the learner to practice cultural colloquialisms.

The key here is developing these specialized programs specifically for these topics through customized LLMs. This approach cannot be easily replicated because deep pedagogical studies and linguistic research must be integrated directly into the code. Each of these features acts as a highly niche mini project that simply cannot be spun up on a standard conversational LLM.

Furthermore, one major way Duolingo can get ahead and truly secure their throne is by continuously asking users what they like and do not like. This is the exact same way Google and OpenAI build their foundation models, training on data to see what users prefer. Every few lessons, the app could ask the user which explanation they prefer, whether they liked a specific audio response, or if a correction was helpful. By building the model based directly on user preferences and collecting data in that specific way, they create a reinforcement feedback loop that a generic AI cannot compete with.

I saw this exact necessity for adaptation play out in real time during my Site Reliability Engineering internship at Carnival Cruise Line. We had a team building an internal infrastructure tool when a new AI solution hit the market doing something very similar. Instead of fighting it or rigidly sticking to the original roadmap, the team immediately pivoted, adapted the architecture, and leveraged the new technology to our advantage.

During my exit interview, my manager gave me a piece of advice that perfectly sums this up. AI is not going to take over all the jobs, but it is going to rapidly evolve the roles of those doing them.

Just some food for thought. I am not a CEO, but as engineers, we can learn a lot from watching how these businesses adapt to the market instead of trying to fight the current.
